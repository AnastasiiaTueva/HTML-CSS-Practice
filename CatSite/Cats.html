The World of Cat Colors:
  • Black cats can temporarily change color. If your cat loves to sunbathe, UV rays can break down the melanin in their fur, bleaching it and giving it a rusty, reddish-brown tint.
  • The high levels of melanin that produce their dark coats heavily dictate their irises as well, which is why almost all black cats have striking, bright yellow or golden eyes.
  • While North American and European folklore often unfairly tie them to bad luck, in Scotland, Ireland, and Japan, black cats are widely considered symbols of prosperity, wealth, and good fortune.
  • Pure white fur isn't a specific breed trait. It is caused by the dominant "W" (White) masking gene, which essentially hides all other potential colors and patterns.
  • White cats are often confused with albino cats. True albinos completely lack melanin, usually resulting in pinkish or pale-blue eyes. White cats, on the other hand, produce melanin and can have stunning blue, green, or gold eyes.
  • Because pigment cells (melanocytes) sometimes don't distribute evenly, many white cats are "odd-eyed" (having one blue eye and one of another color).
  • Male calicos do exist, but they occur in only about 1 out of 3,000 births. They are born with an extra X chromosome (\(XXY\)) and are almost always sterile.
  • Because they are so unique, calicos are global symbols of good fortune.
  • In October 2001, the calico cat was officially named the State Cat of Maryland.
